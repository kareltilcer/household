# 09 — Decision register

Every decision that shaped this specification, with the alternative that was rejected and why.
**A decision without a rejected alternative is a note, not a decision** — entries here always name
what was not chosen.

Referenced throughout as **D-n**.

## Architecture and tenancy

| # | Decision | Rejected alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D-1** | Shared database, `household_id` on every tenant row | Schema-per-tenant; database-per-tenant | Migrations across tens of thousands of schemas become the operational bottleneck; per-database is unrealistic at consumer price points |
| **D-2** | PostgreSQL RLS with `FORCE`, under application scoping. *Under D-93 the replicated path is scoped by the stream definitions generated from the entity registry alone, since PowerSync's replication role bypasses RLS (within D-3's one exception); a read-path isolation test holds them instead* | Application scoping alone | A missing `WHERE` clause returns nothing instead of another family's data. The failure mode is the whole point |
| **D-3** | **No database role bypasses RLS for content.** No impersonation, no support session, no "view as". *D-93 makes one exception, the sync service's own credentials, which no staff member or tool uses: PowerSync's replication role, which bypasses RLS, and the credential to its bucket storage, which holds replicated content outside RLS* | A consented, time-boxed, audited impersonation flow | Makes the no-content-access guarantee a property of the system rather than a policy. Cost: some bugs are slower to diagnose — accepted, mitigated by D-20 |
| **D-4** | Household in the URL path; no "current household" on the session | A header, or session-selected active household | Explicit, visible in logs and traces, cache-friendly, and unambiguous for offline clients holding several households |
| **D-5** | Single EU region, EU subprocessors, no non-EU transfer in the request path | Multi-region from launch | Cleanest GDPR posture for a European consumer product; multi-region doubles the ops surface for no early benefit |
| **D-6** | Export and erase are **mandatory** module interfaces | Optional, added when needed | Retrofitting export across seventeen modules is how it never gets done. Art. 17 and 20 are not negotiable |
| **D-7** | Two credential presentations (web cookie, mobile token pair), one authorization path | Tokens everywhere; cookies everywhere | Cookies are XSS-safe on web and wrong on native; one authorization path means one place to get right |
| **D-8** | The WebSocket carries a **nudge**, not a payload. *Under D-93 replication needs no nudge, and the socket is gone (D-124): every row, a chat message's included, reaches a client on PowerSync's connection* | Per-user payload fan-out (`home` v10's design) | If the payload never rides the socket, the socket never needs to know who may see it. Chat is the single exception |
| **D-9** | Pre-signed URLs per object, minutes-long, never per prefix | Direct bucket access; long-lived URLs | The URL carries the authorization decision, not the authorization |
| **D-10** | Staging never holds production data, anonymised or not | An anonymised production copy | The debugging value is exactly the privacy exposure D-3 exists to prevent |
| **D-11** | Expand/contract migrations, forward-only | In-place breaking migrations | Mobile clients cannot be force-updated; a migration that breaks the previous minor is a broken product |

## Sync

| # | Decision | Rejected alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D-22** | The change-feed row carries `module`, `visibility`, `owner_id`, `audience_id` — the last being any enumerated member list, a chat conversation or a `member_shared` calendar alike. *Under D-93 the replicated path reads no feed row: streams generated from the entity registry read each entity's own table, which keeps the check in one place* | Joining to module tables to authorise a feed pull | One indexed scan with one `WHERE`, whose correctness lives in one place, instead of seventeen joins re-established per module |
| **D-23** | **Client-generated UUIDv7 ids**, mandatory | Server-assigned ids with local-to-real remapping | An offline create must have a stable identity immediately. Remapping across every table is where offline systems go to die |
| **D-24** | Every entity declares one of five merge policies; **no global default**. A `state_set` entity additionally declares its key and its resolution rule; an `additive` entity declares any cross-row invariant it carries | Last-write-wins everywhere; or a policy set broad enough that declaring one says nothing | A shopping item that flickers is a nuisance; a finance rule silently half-merged is a wrong number nobody finds. The two sub-declarations exist because four entities were using `state_set` for things the one-line definition did not cover |
| **D-25** | Attachments upload out of band with a `pending` status on the row | Blocking the mutation on the upload | A receipt photographed in a car park should appear on every device before the bytes have moved |
| **D-26** | Client timestamps are advisory; ordering is by server receipt; skew beyond 24 h is clamped | Trusting client clocks for ordering | A skewed device must not reorder a household's history |
| **D-39** | Conflicts are surfaced for `strict_version` entities and invisible for `lww_field` ones | Always ask; never ask | The dividing line is whether a wrong answer costs anything |
| **D-76** | The activity log is **not** synced offline | Replicating it like everything else | Unbounded, rarely read, never needed offline — the largest local store for the least benefit |
| **D-80** | Permissions are never client-authoritative; cached grants are rendering hints | Trusting the cached grant offline | A client must never believe it can change its own permissions |
| **D-82** | Mandate the **sync-ready schema** in Phase 0 week 1, before choosing an engine | Building the engine first and adding the columns as they are needed | The schema half is catastrophic to change later and cheap to get right; the engine half is contained and replaceable. Separating them is what makes the risk manageable |
| **D-83** | **Spike two off-the-shelf sync engines before building one**, timeboxed to a week with a written verdict | Building from scratch by default | The design is close enough to existing systems that building it should be a decision. Even if the build proceeds, it proceeds having learnt which requirement forced it |
| **D-84** | Offline **writes** ship per merge policy behind a per-entity flag — `additive` and `state_set` everywhere first, plus `lww_field` **for Shopping only**, which is the module the engine is proved on | Shipping offline writes for every entity at once; or holding all `lww_field` to Phase 2 | The two policies that cannot conflict by construction cover the three offline scenarios that sell the product. Shopping is the exception because gate G-C tests it on real phones and a list you cannot add to offline proves nothing. Everywhere else a Phase 1 bug still degrades to "you need signal for that edit" rather than "your data is wrong". This argument only holds while `state_set` genuinely means idempotent, which is why lexorank positions were moved out of it to `lww_field` rather than riding the Phase 1 flag on a technicality |
| **D-85** | Clients send a **rolling digest** of local state; the server forces a resnapshot on mismatch. *Under D-93 PowerSync's bucket checksums hold a replica to its buckets first, and a resnapshot is a re-download; the digest stays, as the replica's report (D-125)* | Trusting the tests and waiting for user reports | Divergence is the bug class unit tests miss and users never report clearly. This turns it into an alerting metric |
| **D-88** | A partly-visible entity emits **two feed rows** — full to the owner, `redacted` to everyone else. A redacted projection carries whatever the busy form needs to be *correct*, including a recurrence rule. *Under D-93 the redacted form is a client table of its own and reaches the owner as well, who is shown the full row over it* | One row filtered or re-rendered per recipient on the way out; or a projection so minimal that a recurring private event renders as a single block | Per-recipient rendering on the pull hot path is the mistake D-8 avoided on the socket. Two rows keep the pull a single indexed scan, and what is safe to reveal stays the entity's decision. A busy block that is wrong about *when* somebody is busy fails at the one job it has, so the cadence travels and the content does not |
| **D-90** | The **audience floor** is a term in the sync pull predicate; the audience membership row stores `floor_seq`. *Under D-93 the floor is the readers the server keeps on each row the audience bounds, and `floor_seq` is not stored* | Filtering chat out of the generic feed into a chat-specific pull that already understands floors | A second delivery path is a second place for the four access axes to be re-implemented. Without the term, the REST path honours FR-CT2's floor and the feed hands a newly added member the conversation's back catalogue |
| **D-91** | Client-generated ids are required on the **REST create path** too, not only in sync mutations, and an architecture test enforces it | Requiring them only where the sync protocol already carries them, and letting REST creates mint server ids | An entity that gets a server id online and a client id offline is exactly the dual identity D-23 exists to prevent. The requirement was already stated; what was missing was the thing that fails the build |
| **D-92** | An `Idempotency-Key` stores only a `2xx` response: a request refused before it took effect stores nothing, and a repeat runs it again. A repeat while the first request runs, or of one that took effect without its response being kept, answers `409 idempotency_in_progress` and is never run again. A first request that has not taken effect five minutes after it arrived is taken to have died: a repeat then runs it, and the first can no longer take effect | Storing every response, refusals included, for the key's seven days; or storing the response only after the handler, with no marker committed beside the effect | A stored refusal would answer a retry after its reason had gone: a `402` after the payment, a `404` after the grant came back. Without the marker, a process that dies between the effect and the store leaves a claim a repeat takes over, and the effect happens twice ([ADR 0006](../adr/0006-sync-ready-schema-and-the-mutation-spine.md)) |
| **D-93** | **Adopt PowerSync, self-hosted, for replication; the write path stays ours.** PowerSync replicates from PostgreSQL into buckets its streams define and keeps the client replica; clients push through `POST …/sync/mutations`, where every mutation goes through the mutation spine. The access predicate ([03](03-platform-strands.md) §2.3) becomes stream definitions generated from the entity registry; an audience's floor (D-90) is a reader set the server keeps on each row, and a redacted projection (D-88) is a client table of its own that reaches the owner as well. Gate G-C's fallback becomes building [03](03-platform-strands.md) §2's engine. Whether the digest (D-85) and the nudge (D-8) keep endpoints of their own beside PowerSync was left to plan item 17: the digest stays (D-125) and the socket goes (D-124). PowerSync's two credentials are the one exception to D-3: its replication role bypasses row-level security, and its bucket storage is household content under [05](05-privacy-and-compliance.md) §6 like the database; both are the service's own, and no staff member or tool connects with either | Building §2's feed pull, snapshot, `retract` rows and client library; Electric (reads only, and its own client ignores the move-outs that carry access loss); Zero (no offline writes); Replicache (maintenance mode) | The spike ([ADR 0001](../adr/0001-sync-engine.md)) ran scenarios 3 and 7 on the sync-ready schema with members' writes through the spine: PowerSync converged, and deleted rows for every cause of access loss it was given, with no client code but a connector. What building would keep, row-level security under the read path and the floor as a term of one predicate, is held instead by generated streams and an isolation test |
| **D-106** | **A mutation's answer is kept under its household, its sender and its `mutation_id`**, with what the mutation carried: a delivery of the same mutation is answered as it was, a `mutation_id` its sender sends again carrying another mutation is `rejected`, and a `deferred` mutation keeps no answer. An answer that took effect is kept in the effect's own transaction | Answers kept per household alone, as FR-SY5 first worded it; the first answer returned to whatever a reused id carries; a kept `deferred` | Keyed on the household alone, a member sending an id another member used would be answered with the other's answer, and its row, which the sender may not see; keyed on the sender too, a member's own retry is still answered as it was. An id carrying another mutation is a client's bug, not its retry, and the first answer would tell it that a write it never made took effect. A deferred mutation took no effect, and what it waited on may since have been put right: a kept `deferred` would hold its replay back for seven days. An answer kept apart from its effect could be lost while the effect stood, and the retry would apply the mutation twice |
| **D-121** | **The mutation spine writes no change feed**: a mutation still reports a change for each row it writes, which the spine checks against the row's entity, and `sync_changes` is no longer written, nor its household lock taken. The table stays until gate G-C passes (item 34 drops it), and the push's answer carries no feed sequence | Keeping the write, with its partitions made ahead and a retention, against a reader to come; or dropping the table in the same release | Under D-93 nothing reads the feed: PowerSync replicates the rows from the write-ahead log. Kept, it was the largest table of all (07 §1: ~4 B rows a year at Year 3) and its lock serialised every household's commits, for no reader. Dropped at once, an instance still running would fail every mutation until it restarted, and G-C's fallback, building [03](03-platform-strands.md) §2's engine, would lose the table it builds on; restoring the write from the change every mutation still reports is cheap while the table stays ([ADR 0018](../adr/0018-sync-engine-ii-versions-visibility-audiences-and-the-feed.md)) |
| **D-122** | **A write is held to the `base_version` it carries against the row as the push locks it**: a `strict_version` update, delete or action names its base version and is answered `conflict`, carrying the row, when the row is at another; an `lww_field` or `lww_row` write made against an older version is applied over the change it had not seen and answered `merged` (`concurrent_change`), carrying the row; and an `lww_row` write behind its row preserves the row it replaced, the loser, in its module. A replica's own earlier write is never a change it had not seen: a later mutation of a batch to a row an earlier one wrote, made against the same version, is held to the version the earlier left, and a client sends a mutation it made while an earlier one of the row was in flight against the version that one's answer returned | `merged` only where a field the client set was overwritten since its base version; a platform table of losers | The server keeps no history of each field's versions to tell which changed; the client holds its own change and the row it is sent back, which is what it decides what to surface by. A loser is household content held to its row's visibility and erased with its module's data, as notes already keep theirs (modules/07 FR-NO10). The activity log keeps a replaced value where the entity records its diffs, so scenario 2's loser is surfaced, not dropped |
| **D-123** | **A rewrite of the access a row carries is not an edit of it**: the readers of the rows an audience bounds, rewritten when its membership changes, and the visibility and owner of the rows a private item bounds, rewritten when it moves between shared and private, leave each row's version, `updated_by` and `updated_at` as they were; the database refuses such a rewrite that changes anything else | Versioning the rewrite as any update; keeping readers in a table of their own, resolved by a subquery in the stream | Versioned, it would turn every queued or `If-Match` edit to a message of a conversation someone left into a conflict or a preserved loser, for a change the member never saw and that changes nothing they read. A reader table was accepted by PowerSync's compiler only alone, never beside the grant's subquery ([ADR 0001](../adr/0001-sync-engine.md)) |
| **D-124** | **The realtime socket is removed** (`…/stream`): a chat message reaches its readers through its stream on PowerSync's connection, a member's grants on the membership row, and the household's entitlement on the household's row, whose stream sends its state, its clocks and its restriction as its banner shows them | Keeping the socket for Chat's payloads, `entitlement_changed` and `access_changed` | PowerSync's connection carries every row as it changes, which removes the pull round-trip that made Chat the exception to D-8; grants and the entitlement are state on rows a replica already holds, and a second channel would carry them a second way, to be kept in step |
| **D-125** | **The digest is the replica's report**, sent at rest: per entity type, the rows it holds and an order-independent hash of their `(entity_id, version)` pairs, and its health (checkpoint, queue, unresolved conflicts and rejections, bucket checksum failures). The server computes the same over what the caller may see as the report arrives; a mismatch is divergence only when the next report, a minute or more later, mismatches again while the server's own hash held still. Divergence, or an owner's request, has the replica download itself again once its queue has drained | Retiring the digest, since PowerSync checks each bucket's checksum; comparing at the client's cursor, as D-85 first said | Checksums hold a replica to its buckets, never the buckets to PostgreSQL: a replication fault, or a stream that disagrees with its entity's declared access, passes them all. A PowerSync client has no feed cursor, and PostgreSQL keeps no row's history to compare at; two reports either side of a minute in which the server's rows held still tell divergence from a household being written to. A re-download that discarded a queue would lose what nobody synced |
| **D-126** | **A `member_shared` calendar's audience is readers kept on every row it bounds**, as a conversation's, rewritten when a member joins or leaves | Resolving it through the calendar's member list in the stream, since it has no floor | The member list beside the grant's subquery was never tried, and the conformance suite proves one mechanism; one way to bound a row is one place to get right. A calendar with years of events makes a join or a departure a large rewrite, which item 90 measures |
| **D-128** | **A replica's report is kept 90 days past the last one, and compared on what it names**: it is its member's, and leaves with their membership before that; an entity type's rows counted once by id across its own client table and its redacted projection's; an entity type the replica does not report not compared; one the server does not sync answered as disagreeing and never as divergence; a replica told to download itself again shown as needing it until the report it sends after | Keeping a replica's report as long as its household, or until its device is revoked; comparing every entity type the server syncs, reported or not | An app uninstalled or a browser cleared never reports again, and a sync-health screen listing it for ever says nothing about the replicas that are there; a season unused is what a phone left in a drawer looks like. An app older than the server subscribes to fewer streams, and would be told to download itself again for ever; a redacted projection is the same entity's rows, which a replica and the server can each count once ([ADR 0019](../adr/0019-the-sync-client-library.md)) |
| **D-129** | **An edit merges into its row's queued mutation while that mutation has not been sent**, keeping its id and the version it was made against; once it may have been sent, a later edit is a mutation of its own, sent against the version the earlier one's answer returned (D-122). A column the server sets is written for the replica alone and never sent | Every edit its own mutation; merging a row's queued writes into one as a batch is formed | 06-clients §5's pending row is fully editable and its edits merge, which keeps a week offline from becoming thousands of mutations against the household's day (D-127), and a deferred write of a row from replaying behind the row's own later edits. A batch whose answer was lost is sent again unchanged; merged as it was formed, it would carry another mutation under a `mutation_id` already sent, which FR-SY5 refuses |

## Identity and access

| # | Decision | Rejected alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D-12** | Breached-password screening against a local dataset | A third-party API check | No password material, hashed or prefixed, leaves the platform |
| **D-13** | Enumeration-resistant sign-up and reset (always `202`) | Telling the user the email is taken | Account enumeration is the first step of every credential-stuffing campaign |
| **D-14** | Rotating single-use refresh tokens with family reuse detection | Long-lived refresh tokens | Standard detection for a stolen token, and cheap |
| **D-15** | Access tokens carry **no** roles or grants | Embedding grants in the JWT | Revoking access takes effect on the next request, not in fifteen minutes |
| **D-16** | Refusals are `404`, never `403`, for grants and privacy alike | `403` for "exists but forbidden" | A `403` is an existence oracle — over private items, over conversations, and over which modules a household uses |
| **D-17** | A child is a **managed sub-profile**, not a self-registering user | A real account with verifiable parental consent | Age of digital consent varies 13–16 across the EU; the parent is present, is the customer, and is already administering the account |
| **D-18** | Child profiles are **excluded** from analytics, not anonymised within it | Anonymised inclusion | Exclusion is checkable; anonymisation is a claim |
| **D-19** | Adults' private roots are readable by nobody; a child's is readable by an owner, and the child is told | Symmetric privacy; or silent parental access | A private space a parent can *silently* read is worse than no private space |
| **D-20** | Member-initiated diagnostic bundles, shown in full before sending | Support-initiated data pulls | The member decides, and sees what they shared |
| **D-59** | Finance defaults to `none` for new members | `home`'s posture, where a reader saw both incomes | Flatmates, adult children and separated co-parents all exist. The default must be closed |
| **D-78** | A member is notified when their access changes | Silent grant changes | An access change discovered by finding something missing is a bug |
| **D-95** | A web session lasts **30 days from its last use, with no absolute maximum** | A 90-day cap from sign-in; or a shorter 14-day idle, 30-day maximum | A household app is opened daily for years. A cap signs members out on a date that means nothing to them, and a stolen cookie is ended by the session list, signing out everywhere, or a password reset, not by waiting |
| **D-96** | The limits §9 did not first give: resending a verification email **once a minute and five times an hour** per address; the note a registration sends an address that already has an account **three times an hour** per address, past which the registration answers `202` and sends nothing; asking for a reset, and resending, **twenty times an hour** each per IP, whichever addresses it names; a household's API budget **3 000 a minute, burst 500**, shared by its members; the login cooldown **starting at a minute and doubling to an hour**; both sign-in limits count failed attempts, not every attempt | Resend at 3 an hour, as a reset; the note limited only by the network's five registrations an hour, or refused `429` per address; a reset and a resend limited per address alone, as §9 first gave them; a household budget equal to one member's, or none until item 90; a fixed 15-minute cooldown | The design's resend countdown is about a minute, and five an hour caps the mail one address can trigger. The network's limit alone lets registrations from many networks fill one mailbox, and a `429` per address would refuse a new address's owner for someone else's attempts; a note left unsent tells the caller nothing. Each reset and resend queues a lookup that runs after the response: limited per address alone, one client naming a new address each time is never held back, and its lookups fill the queue every other email waits in, which then drops them; twenty an hour is more than a household or an office asks for. A household's budget of about five busy members keeps one member from using the others' up without throttling a normal family. A doubling cooldown lets a person who mistyped back in within a minute and holds a guessing script to a few attempts an hour. Counting failures only means a family signing in on one network is never refused |
| **D-97** | **A request before sign-in keeps no `Idempotency-Key`, nor does one whose body carries a password**: registering, verifying an address, resending, signing in, asking for or confirming a reset, and changing a password check the header and store nothing, and a repeat runs again. A request that ended the session it was made with (signing out, signing out everywhere, revoking the current session) keeps its key, but a repeat of it answers `401` | Keys held by the key alone for anonymous callers; or by the address the request names; a signed-in password change keeping its key on the account like every other account request; an ended session still presenting its key, to be answered from it | There is no caller whose key it could be. A stored sign-in would store a credential, or answer without the cookie it needs; a fingerprint of a body that carries a password is a fast hash of the password, kept for a week beside its Argon2id hash. Each of these operations is safe to repeat as it stands; a repeated password change answers `401`, the current password being the new one. A session that still answered, if only from its keys, would not have ended ([ADR 0009](../adr/0009-accounts-sessions-throttles-and-the-breach-corpus.md)) |
| **D-98** | **A refresh token presented again within a minute of its use, while the token it was exchanged for is still unused, is a retry whose answer was lost**, answered with a new pair; the unused one is retired, so presenting it later is a reuse. Any other second use revokes the family (D-14) | D-14 to the letter: every second use revokes the family | A phone on a bad connection loses the answer to a refresh and retries with the token it still holds. Taken for theft, that signs the member out, emails them an alarm and discards the replica with its unsent changes. A thief who beats the device to a token inside the minute is found out at the device's next refresh, at most fifteen minutes later, when the token the device holds turns out retired. Auth0 and Okta keep the same leeway ([ADR 0010](../adr/0010-mobile-tokens-second-step-providers-and-client-versions.md)) |
| **D-99** | **A device's sign-in lasts until it is revoked**, with no idle expiry: by signing out on it, revoking it, signing out everywhere, a password reset, a password change made on another client, signing in on it again, or a reused refresh token | 30 days from the last refresh, as a web session (D-95); or 90 days | The please-update screen (A-21) is written for a phone that has been in a drawer for a year, and promises its unsent changes are still waiting. An expired sign-in would discard them. A refresh token still rotates on every use and a reused one still revokes the family, so a copied token is caught; a lost phone is revoked from the device list |
| **D-100** | **The second step is asked of every browser and device not trusted**: trust is opt-in when the step is answered, and lasts 30 days (A-7). **Ten wrong codes since the last right one lock the authenticator**, and end the account's challenges; a locked authenticator takes only a recovery code, which unlocks it, or support's unlock (item 21). Every recovery code spent is emailed (A-8). A password reset keeps the second step on and ends every trust; turning it off, or on again, ends every trust too, and so do signing a device or a session out from its list and a reused refresh token, since what was signed out may be lost with its trust in it. **Only an account whose address is verified turns the second step on** | A new device meaning one never seen by the account, by a client-chosen id; a lock that a password reset lifts; a lock that lapses after a time; an unverified account turning it on, and a reset that proves the address turning it off | A device id is the client's own and names no secret, so a trust has to be a token the browser or device holds. A lock a reset lifts would make the mailbox the only factor, which is the case the second step exists for; one that lapses gives a guesser a fresh ten each time. A recovery code has forty bits, so it cannot be guessed through the lock, and the legitimate owner always has a way back. Someone who registers another person's address could otherwise bind their own authenticator before the address is proven, and the reset that hands the owner the account (D-102) would keep it; turning it off at such a reset would instead let the mailbox alone past a second step its owner set up |
| **D-101** | Two limits §9 did not give: **five wrong second-step codes in five minutes per account**, which bounds every challenge it has and the first code that turns the step on; and **sixty sign-ins begun with a provider an hour per IP** | A limit per challenge alone; no limit on the first code, since enrolling takes the password; no limit on beginning, since a start is anonymous | A challenge belongs to one account, so the account's limit bounds all of them at once, and new challenges cost a password check each. The first code is answered with the recovery codes, and an enrolment its owner left waiting never expires: unlimited, a stolen session alone could guess its way to them. Each start writes a row that waits ten minutes for its callback: unlimited, one client could fill the table; sixty an hour is more than a family on one network begins |
| **D-102** | **A provider's identity whose address an account already has is never linked on its own, whether or not that account verified the address**: the sign-in answers `409 link_required`, and the account's owner links it signed in. A new account takes the provider's address, verified only when the provider verified it. **A password reset that proves an address nobody had proven unlinks every provider the account held before it** | Linking to a verified account after a confirmation screen within the sign-in; creating a second account beside an unverified one; a reset that leaves the providers linked before it in place | FR-ID2 forbids a silent link to a verified account, and an unverified account may be a squatter's, registered with someone else's address before its owner arrived: linking the owner's Google to it would sign them into an account the squatter still holds. A password reset proves the mailbox and ends the squatter's sessions, and the owner links from there; a provider the squatter linked while the address was unproven would outlive the reset and sign them back in, so the reset unlinks it, and an owner who had linked their own links it again. An address the provider did not vouch for is no more proven than a typed one |
| **D-103** | **An invitation proposes an owner or a member, never a child**; an email invitation is accepted only by the verified account with its address, a link by up to its `max_uses`, at most 12; sending one again sends a new link and reopens a declined, withdrawn or expired one; **an owner's invitations lapse with their ownership**, what they sent that is still waiting being withdrawn when they are removed, leave or are made a member. **The member list, every member's grants, the household's settings and its modules are read by every member, a child included**; its invitations with `view` on Household settings. **The payer stays an owner** until billing has moved, and an owner made a member keeps the levels they held | Inviting a child, as design/v1's composer offers; accepting an email invitation from any account holding its link; an owner's invitations working on after they are removed, leave or are made a member; the member list and the settings behind `view` on Household settings, as FR-AC3 first read; demoting the payer and leaving billing with a member | A child profile is a managed object without an address (D-17): an invitation would make a self-registered account a child.
